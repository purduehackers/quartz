## Overview
Over the past few years, we’ve built a community that reliably brings highly talented builders to the room. We know how to get people to show up, how to make events feel real instead of transactional, and how to create environments where ambitious students genuinely engage. Companies have started to notice. When Neuralink showed up, they stayed until 6am. Sentry is coming because they see people they’d actually want to hire. We’re fielding requests to come to campus from Vercel, Pierre, Retro, and more.
What’s already happening is simple: we’re informally becoming the best interface between certain companies and Purdue’s talent community. B2B Hackers exists to formalize and scale that.
Today, Purdue Hackers is funded through a mix of sponsorships, donations, and one-off partnerships. That works, but it’s unpredictable and limits how ambitious we can be. At the same time, companies spend significant time and money trying to recruit students here, and much of it is wasted. They table in Lawson, hand out shitty swag, and leave with a pile of resumes but very little signal. The university adds another layer of friction through bureaucracy that most external companies don’t understand and don’t want to deal with. We’ve seen this with the Corporate Partners program dropping from 64 members to just 14 semi-active members.
We sit in the middle with an unusual advantage. We have distribution and taste. We know who in our community actually builds. We know how to get those people into a room, how to present something in a way that doesn’t feel like a career fair, and how to make interactions feel worth someone’s time. That combination is rare.
B2B Hackers is about capturing that value without changing what makes Purdue Hackers work.
In practice, this can go in two directions.
First, we help companies build a real presence within Purdue Hackers and the broader CS ecosystem. Not through spammy announcements or email chains, but by integrating them into things that already matter. When a company shows up through us, they don’t feel like an outsider trying to recruit. They feel like they’re part of something people already want to be at. That shift alone changes how students engage with them. Think about the excitement that people in our community had when Neuralink was coming to Purdue, in part because Kartavya interned there and got others excited about what they do. Even after they left campus there was a buzz within Purdue Hackers when talking about Neuralink.
Second, we treat recruitment like an operational problem that can be solved well. Most companies approach campus recruiting as a series of disconnected events. We approach it as a system. We build awareness before they arrive, shape how they’re perceived, fill rooms with the right people, and run events that actually convert into relationships. We also handle the parts companies struggle with, like navigating Purdue’s systems and getting access to the right channels. The result is not just more applicants, but better ones, with stronger context and higher intent.
We’ve already seen this work. The goal now is to make it repeatable and to do it at a larger scale with more partners.
This only works because of Purdue Hackers itself. The community is the product. If we degrade it, the entire thing collapses. So B2B Hackers has to operate with a clear constraint: it cannot make the experience worse for builders. Events cannot turn into recruiting funnels. Our Discord cannot become spam. Companies we work with need to meet a bar, not just in prestige, but in genuine interest in builders.
If anything, this should make the community better. More funding means more µgrants, better events, better infrastructure, and more room for ambitious projects. The goal is not to extract value from the community, but to reinvest it in ways that compound.
Operationally, this should stay small and high-agency: a few people who can close, design, and execute. Not a committee, and not a slow process. The work is straightforward, but it requires taste and speed: finding the right companies, pitching them well, and delivering consistently once they say yes.
If this works, the outcome is simple. Purdue Hackers becomes financially independent in a real way. Instead of relying on one-off sponsorships, we have a steady flow of partners who see clear value in working with us. Companies start coming inbound because they know we can deliver signal. And internally, we get to fund the things we’ve always wanted to do without compromise.
At a high level, B2B Hackers is this: we already know how to bring the right people together and make something happen. Now we’re making that capability legible, repeatable, and valuable enough that others will pay for it.
